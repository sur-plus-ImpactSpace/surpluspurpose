SDG 12 and the R-strategies: where in-kind donations actually count — and why the statistics miss them

Few frameworks are cited as often in sustainability communication as the SDGs, and few as loosely. This guide sorts it out: what the 17 goals legally are, where an in-kind donation actually counts (target 12.5), why the official SDG statistics still miss it, and how the R-strategies supply the precise vocabulary that turns good intent into evidence.

The SDGs are attractive for corporate communication precisely because nobody audits the reference, and that is exactly why the reference alone proves nothing. What does prove something is the combination this guide walks through: the target that names reuse, the indicator that ignores it, and the R-ladder that gives your donation its correct, defensible place.

What the SDGs are — and what they are not

The Sustainable Development Goals come from resolution A/RES/70/1 ("Transforming our world: the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development"), adopted by the UN General Assembly on 25 September 2015: 17 goals, 169 targets, horizon 2030. The addressees are states, not companies.

  • They are: a political goal system with its own set of indicators, the shared frame of reference for governments, NGOs and, increasingly, tenders and funding programmes.
  • They are not: a law, a reporting standard or a certification basis. A General Assembly resolution is not a treaty; no direct corporate obligation follows from it. The obligations around surplus and disposal live elsewhere — in the ESPR destruction ban, in national waste law, in unfair-commercial-practices law.

Target 12.5: the sentence that means in-kind donations

SDG 12 ("Responsible Consumption and Production") contains the target that directly concerns passing surplus on. Target 12.5 reads:

"By 2030, substantially reduce waste generation through prevention, reduction, recycling and reuse."

Reuse stands there expressly, on equal footing with recycling. An in-kind donation of functional goods (office furniture, IT, returns, excess stock) is exactly that: a product keeps its function and merely changes its user. A company that donates instead of discarding is not acting "in the spirit" of the 2030 Agenda; it is acting along the literal wording of 12.5.

The measurement gap: reuse is statistically invisible

Now the uncomfortable half of the truth. Every target has official indicators against which UN statistics measure progress. For 12.5 there is exactly one: indicator 12.5.1, "National recycling rate, tons of material recycled". It counts recycling, composting and anaerobic digestion. Reuse is not recorded.

The result is a paradox worth knowing before you put SDG references into a report: the target names four routes, the indicator measures one. A company that passes a thousand pieces of furniture to nonprofit recipients for continued use improves its country’s SDG statistics by exactly zero tonnes. Had it shredded the same furniture for material recovery, the tonnage would have counted as recycling, although that is the environmentally worse tier.

What follows from this. The SDG indicator set is unusable as evidence of reuse, not because donating does not count, but because it is not counted. To evidence the contribution you need your own per-transaction material-flow records: category, quantity or weight, destination, recipient, date. What that dataset looks like is covered in the guide to in-kind donations and ESRS E5.

The R-ladder: from R0 Refuse to R9 Recover

Where the SDGs supply the why, the R-strategies supply the how, a ranking of circularity strategies ordered by value retention. The most cited version is by Potting, Hekkert, Worrell and Hanemaaijer (2017), published by the Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency (PBL): ten strategies from R0 to R9 in three groups. The ground rule: the lower the number, the higher the value retention.

StrategyCoreRelation to the donation
R0RefuseMake the product redundant, avoid raw material useActs before the surplus exists — prevents it rather than recovering it
R1RethinkIntensify use (sharing, multifunctional design)
R2ReduceResource efficiency in design and manufacture
R3Re-useContinued use of an intact product by another user, same functionThe in-kind donation of functional goods — this exact definition
R4RepairRepair defects, retain functionUpstream steps: turn defective stock into donatable goods
R5RefurbishRestore and bring up to date
R6RemanufactureParts into a new product, same function
R7RepurposeProduct or parts used in a different functionThe donation with a change of function at the recipient
R8RecycleMaterial recovery into secondary raw materialOnly once continued use is no longer possible
R9RecoverEnergy recovery

A note for anyone citing this: several R-lists circulate with diverging numbering: Potting et al. themselves combine two older systems, and other authors work with seven or nine strategies and assign the same numbers differently. If you write "R3", name the source of your numbering, or the reference cannot be checked.

Where the donation sits: R3 — and sometimes R7

The definition of R3 re-use (continued use of a product in good condition by another user, in its original function) describes an in-kind donation of functional goods word for word. The desk stays a desk, the laptop stays a laptop; only the user changes. If the recipient uses the product or its parts for a different purpose, it is R7 repurpose — also a high-retention strategy, two tiers above recycling.

Which yields the most important distinction in this guide: donating is not recycling. Reporting donated furniture as "recycled" makes two mistakes at once: it downgrades your own measure in the value hierarchy, and it describes it incorrectly, because recycling presupposes the material processing of waste. Donated goods, precisely, never become waste.

From framework to law: the waste hierarchy

The R-ladder is analysis, not law. But it has a legal mirror: the five-tier waste hierarchy in Article 4(1) of Waste Framework Directive 2008/98/EC — (a) prevention, (b) preparing for re-use, (c) recycling, (d) other recovery, (e) disposal — transposed in Germany in section 6 of the Kreislaufwirtschaftsgesetz, which also gives priority to the option that best protects human health and the environment across the whole life cycle.

The point that is almost always missed in practice: transferring functional goods for continued use is re-use within the meaning of Article 3(13) and therefore prevention under Article 3(12): tier (a), before the goods ever become waste. "Preparing for re-use" on tier (b), by contrast, covers only items that have already become waste. A timely donation keeps the surplus out of the waste regime altogether — with the documentation consequences described in the guide to the EU destruction ban: for unsold consumer products, Article 25 of the ESPR (EU) 2024/1781 turns the recommendation into an obligation logic with records.

From mapping to evidence: SDG, ESRS, VSME

This separates the three levels that get mixed constantly in practice:

LevelFrameFunction
CommunicationSDG mappingShows which goals your actions serve — legitimate and useful, as long as every mapping is concretely evidenced: "Our in-kind donations contribute to 12.5: in 2026, X kg were transferred for reuse."
EvidenceESRS E5 / VS B7The auditable material flow per transaction — covered for reporting undertakings in the guide to ESRS E5, including the Voluntary Standard’s disclosure B7 for smaller companies.
OperationsR-strategiesThe decision logic before every disposal: check R3 first (usable by someone else?), then R4–R7 (repairable, repurposable?), only then R8. As a process rule: "No container before the reuse check is documented."

Follow that order and the SDG communication comes free: it is then merely the summary of what the evidence dataset already proves, instead of a separate, uncovered layer of claims.

The limits: what not to claim

From 27 September 2026 environmental claims face a considerably stricter frame: the EmpCo Directive (EU) 2024/825 (transposition deadline 27 March 2026, national rules applying from 27 September 2026) classes generic environmental claims such as "environmentally friendly", "green" or "sustainable" without recognised excellent environmental performance as unfair commercial practices, as is advertising with legal requirements as if they were distinctive features. Check the transposition status in your member state before a campaign.

  • No "SDG-compliant", no "SDG-certified": the United Nations does not certify companies. There is no compliance to invoke — only the evidenced, specific contribution.
  • No invented rates: a "reuse rate" without a documented denominator is contestable. Report absolute, evidenced quantities.
  • No CO₂ figures without methodology: avoided emissions from reuse can be calculated, but only with disclosed methodology, and reported separately from your own inventory.
  • No "climate-neutral through donating": a donation offsets nothing. It prevents waste and extends product life, which is its evidenced, entirely sufficient value.

Frequently asked questions

Are the SDGs legally binding for companies?

No. The 2030 Agenda is a UN General Assembly resolution (A/RES/70/1 of 25 September 2015) addressed to states: it creates no direct corporate obligations. Binding duties around surplus and disposal come from other sources: Article 4 of Waste Framework Directive 2008/98/EC (in Germany transposed in section 6 KrWG) and, for unsold consumer products, Article 25 of the ESPR (EU) 2024/1781.

Does an in-kind donation count towards SDG 12?

Yes: target 12.5 names reuse expressly as one of four routes to reducing waste generation. The official indicator 12.5.1, however, measures only the national recycling rate in tonnes; reuse does not enter it. To evidence the contribution you therefore need your own per-transaction records: category, quantity or weight, destination, recipient.

Is donating goods recycling?

No. In the R-framework of Potting et al. (2017), passing on functional goods is re-use (R3), not recycling (R8). Legally it sits even earlier: transfer for continued use is re-use under Article 3(13) of Directive 2008/98/EC and therefore prevention under Article 3(12), the goods never become waste. Reporting donations as recycled downgrades your own measure and describes it incorrectly.

May we advertise as SDG-compliant?

There is no such thing as SDG compliance, the United Nations does not certify companies and issues no label. From 27 September 2026 the EmpCo Directive (EU) 2024/825 in addition tightens the rules on environmental advertising: generic claims such as sustainable or environmentally friendly are unfair without recognised excellent environmental performance. What stays safe is specificity: quantity, destination, recipient, year.

Which R-number does our donation have?

Usually R3 (re-use): another organisation uses the product, in good condition, for its original function. If the product or its parts serve a different function at the recipient, it is R7 (repurpose). When citing, note that several R-lists with diverging numbering circulate; always name the source of your numbering; Potting et al. 2017 is the usual reference.
